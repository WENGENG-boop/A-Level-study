

## 1. Key Determinants of Market Structure

To understand any market, economists look at these four criteria:

- Number of firms: Is there one firm, a few, or many?
    
- Barriers to entry: How hard is it for new firms to join the industry? (e.g., high start-up costs, patents).
    
- Product differentiation: Are the goods identical (homogeneous) or branded?
    
- Price power: Does the firm set the price (Price Maker) or follow the market (Price Taker)?

## 2. Competitive Market (Perfect Competition)

In a perfectly competitive market, many small firms compete against each other.

### Characteristics:

- Many buyers and sellers: No single firm can influence the market price.
    
- Homogeneous products: Products are identical (e.g., gold, wheat).
    
- No barriers to entry/exit: New firms can enter easily if they see profits being made.
    
- Perfect information: Buyers and sellers know everything about prices and quality.
    

### Advantages & Disadvantages:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Advantages|Disadvantages|
|Lower Prices: Intense competition pushes prices down for consumers.|Lack of Innovation: Low profits mean firms have no money for Research & Development (R&D).|
|Efficiency: Firms must be efficient to survive; wasteful firms go out of business.|No Variety: Since products are identical, consumers have no choice in style or features.|
|Responsive to Consumers: Firms produce what consumers want to stay in business.|No Economies of Scale: Firms stay small and cannot lower average costs through mass production.|

## 3. Monopoly

A pure monopoly exists when one firm controls the entire market (100% share), though in exams, it often refers to any firm with significant market power.

### Characteristics:

- Single seller: One firm dominates the industry.
    
- Unique product: No close substitutes available.
    
- High barriers to entry: Legal (patents), capital (huge costs), or natural (control of resources).
    
- Price Maker: The firm can increase prices by restricting supply.
    

### Advantages & Disadvantages:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Advantages|Disadvantages|
|Economies of Scale: Large-scale production can lead to lower average costs, which could lead to lower prices.|High Prices: Monopolies often restrict supply to charge higher prices and maximize profit.|
|Research & Development: High "Abnormal Profits" allow the firm to invest in new technology/medicine.|Poor Quality: Without competition, there is no incentive to improve the product or customer service.|
|Natural Monopoly Efficiency: For utilities like water or electricity, it is cheaper to have one set of pipes than many competing ones.|Allocative Inefficiency: They produce less than what society actually needs.|

## 4. Summary Comparison Table

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Feature|Competitive Market|Monopoly|
|Number of Firms|Very Many|One / Dominant|
|Price|Low (Market determined)|High (Firm determined)|
|Output (Quantity)|High|Low|
|Profits|Normal (low) in long run|Abnormal (high) in long run|
|Consumer Choice|Limited (identical goods)|Limited (no substitutes)|

## 5. Exam Tips for Chapter 23

- The "Normal vs. Abnormal Profit" Distinction: Remember that competitive firms only make enough to stay in business (Normal Profit), while Monopolies make "extra" profit (Abnormal Profit).
    
- Barriers to Entry: Be ready to list examples like Patents, Advertising/Brand Loyalty, or High Capital Costs.
    
- The "Double-Sided" Argument: If a question asks "Are monopolies always bad?", always mention Economies of Scale and R&D as the potential benefits.
